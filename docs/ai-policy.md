# AI Use Policy

## Course-Specific Override of University AI Policy

This course explicitly overrides the university's default AI use policy. Because the subject
matter of this course *is* generative AI applied to physics research, blanket restrictions on
AI tool use would be counterproductive. The policy below applies to all work submitted in this
course.

---

## The Core Principle: Intellectual Ownership

> **You are encouraged to use generative AI for everything you feel comfortable using generative
> AI for, but you are expected to be able to demonstrate intellectual ownership and scientific
> understanding.**

This means two things in practice:

1. **You can defend every statement you submit.** If your paper, notebook, or presentation
   claims that a particular model achieves a certain accuracy, or that a physical quantity
   behaves in a certain way, you must be prepared to explain *why* — independently of whatever
   the AI told you.  Any question about what your submitted materials claim is fair game during
   in-class discussion, code review, the showcase Q&A, or any other evaluation moment.

2. **You own the scientific narrative.** Generative AI is a tool, like NumPy or LaTeX.
   The scientific argument — the choice of question, the interpretation of results, the
   judgment of what is physically meaningful — belongs to you.

---

## What This Looks Like in Practice

### Permitted uses (non-exhaustive)

- Drafting and iterating on code, then verifying and understanding what was generated.
- Using an LLM to explain an unfamiliar algorithm or paper before diving into the primary source.
- Generating boilerplate (plots, configuration files, LaTeX tables) that you then inspect and
  correct.
- Using AI-assisted search (e.g., Perplexity, Elicit) to find literature — followed by reading
  the actual papers.
- Discussing physical intuition with a chatbot as a sounding board, then checking against
  textbooks or literature.
- Using agentic workflows (the core skill of this course) as an integral part of your research
  pipeline.

### Things you are responsible for regardless of AI use

- The **correctness of your physics.** If an LLM tells you the virial theorem applies in a
  context where it doesn't, and you include that claim in your paper, that is an error in your
  paper.
- The **reproducibility of your code.** "The AI wrote it and it seemed to work" is not a
  sufficient explanation during the Week 10 code review.
- The **citations in your report.** AI-generated bibliographies frequently contain hallucinated
  references. Every citation must resolve to a real, accessible source that you have at least
  skimmed.
- The **interpretation of results.** Figures, tables, and numerical outputs require a physical
  narrative that you can articulate without assistance.

---

## Disclosure and Transparency

You are not required to disclose every prompt you wrote. You *are* required to:

- **Acknowledge AI-assisted code generation** in your repository's README (a brief sentence
  is sufficient, e.g., *"Portions of this code were developed with GitHub Copilot and
  Claude."*).
- **Note substantial AI contributions to written text** in your final paper, consistent with
  the emerging norms described in the Week 10 ethics module and the journals relevant to your
  subfield. When in doubt, disclose more rather than less.
- **Never fabricate a human contribution.** Submitting AI-generated text or code under the
  pretense that it was entirely human-produced, especially when directly asked, is academic
  dishonesty.

---

## A Note on Assessment Design

The assessments in this course are deliberately structured so that AI cannot do the work for
you end-to-end:

- The **mini-assignments** are graded on methodology; an AI that produces a working script
  does not demonstrate that *you* understand the boundary conditions or failure modes.
- The **proposal pitch** requires you to articulate scope and feasibility live, in front of
  the class.
- The **code review** is peer-to-peer and conversational; you will be asked to explain
  specific design choices.
- The **showcase Q&A** is five minutes of unscripted questions from faculty and fellow
  students.

In each case, the assessment moment is one that rewards genuine understanding over polished
output.

---

## Summary

| Situation | Expectation |
|-----------|-------------|
| Using AI to write code | ✅ Fine — understand and be able to explain what it does |
| Using AI to draft text | ✅ Fine — revise it, own it, disclose it appropriately |
| Submitting AI output you haven't verified | ⚠️ Risky — errors in the output are errors in your submission |
| Citing a source you haven't checked exists | ❌ Not acceptable — every citation must be real |
| Being unable to explain a claim in your own work | ❌ Not acceptable — intellectual ownership is non-negotiable |
| Claiming purely human authorship when asked directly | ❌ Academic dishonesty |

If you have questions about whether a specific use is appropriate, ask — in class, over email,
or in office hours. When in doubt, the safest path is transparency.
